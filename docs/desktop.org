** The bar's right half, and its chevron

The right half carries what is read all day: what is playing and what is copied,
the applications this desktop opens, the hardware, the network, the tray, the
bell, and then search and the clock at the far right where a menu bar puts them.
Behind the chevron at the end of that row sit the readings that are looked at
once in a while: the load, the keyboard reference and the two browsers of the
desktop's own collections, the keyboard layout and the brightness.

The modules behind the chevron are built with the bar, so they keep polling and
their clicks keep working while the shelf is shut; the chevron only decides
whether they are on screen.  It starts closed - an open shelf eats the width it
was folded away to save - and ~#:overflow-expanded? #t~ in ~whelk.scm~ opens it
with the session.

The split is whelk's own bar's, and it is two lists in ~src/heks-linux/whelk.scm~:
~whelk-rows~ is the row and ~whelk-overflow-rows~ is the shelf.  Moving a reading
across is cutting its form out of one list and pasting it into the other, and a
machine that wants the shelf gone empties ~whelk-overflow-rows~ rather than
hiding the chevron - whelk draws it when there is something behind it.

** The desktop's cards

whelk's Golden Gate widgets are on by default: the clock, the calendar, the
weather, the batteries, the notes, the reminders, the world clock and the stocks
sit down the left and the middle of the screen, on the bottom layer, under the
windows rather than over them.  They are a starting layout, not a fixed one:

- the grip on a card drags it, snapped to an eight-pixel grid, and the position
  is remembered per monitor in ~$XDG_STATE_HOME/whelk/widgets-settings.ini~, so
  the laptop panel and the desk screen each keep their own arrangement,
- every card's clicks and scroll directions are commands from ~whelk.scm~, so a
  Notes card can open the note in an editor and a Weather card can run a script,
- ~whelk-widgets~ in ~src/heks-linux/whelk.scm~ is the whole list, one
  ~(desktop-widget ...)~ form per card, and the rendered ~settings.scm~ carries
  the same eight forms: change a card's ~#:x~, ~#:y~, ~#:width~, ~#:height~ or its
  options there, delete its form to leave the card off the desktop, and delete
  the ~#:widgets~ keyword to have none at all, and
- the values are whelk's own defaults, so the cards land where whelk's own
  desktop puts them: the weather is San Francisco because the collection is named
  for the Golden Gate, and the clock, calendar and battery open GNOME's own
  utilities - each of those is one string in that form.

The shell's own states follow from the same file: five idle minutes lock the
session through whelk's locker, and the clipboard keeps a bounded history that
forgets unpinned copies when the session locks and expires them after an hour.

** The glass, and what the compositor blurs

A Wayland surface cannot blur what is behind it - the layer-shell protocol has
no backdrop filter - so the shell paints a thin translucent veil and the
compositor blurs the desktop under it.  The rules that do it are in
~src/heks-linux/niri.scm~, and they name each surface by the namespace whelk
gives it, which is what ~niri msg layers~ prints:

| *Namespace*                | *Surface*                         |
|----------------------------+-----------------------------------|
| ~whelk-rust~               | the bar                           |
| ~whelk-rust-launcher~      | the launcher                      |
| ~whelk-rust-notifications~ | the notification centre           |
| ~whelk-rust-toasts~        | the notification toast stack      |
| ~whelk-rust-network~       | the Wi-Fi and Bluetooth panel     |
| ~whelk-rust-osd~           | the volume and brightness display |
| ~whelk-widget~             | each desktop card                 |

The bar keeps Niri's automatic ~xray~ mode; the launcher and the panes turn it
off, because they drop over a window and the window is what their glass should
show.  The wallpaper surface (~whelk-rust-wallpaper~) is the one whelk layer
left without a rule: blurring it would give the glass behind it nothing to show.
Every surface here is translucent because its background carries the palette's
opacity, and it wears the material ~whelk.scm~ gives the bar; an opaque palette
or a ~whelk-extra-css~ rule that paints a solid background turns the blur
invisible without turning it off.

The corner radius is one number in two programs: whelk rounds the surface with
~whelk-bar-radius~ (the launcher with ~whelk-window-radius~), and the rule
rounds the blur and the shadow with the same value, so a machine that changes
one gets the other.  A rule at a different radius leaves a square patch of blur
showing past a rounded corner.  ~heks-linux-blur? #f~ turns every one of these
effects off and says so in the generated config, rather than dropping the rules.

** Switching the palette at runtime

The palette picker is the fastest way to see what "your system is a program"
means. Three ways in, all of them the same code path:

- click the palette glyph on the bar (a palette icon, painted in the current
  accent),
- press ~Mod+Shift+C~,
- or run ~maak install-theme~ yourself after editing
  ~$XDG_CONFIG_HOME/heks-linux/local-settings.scm~.

The first two open the palette view of heks-toon, where every palette is a card
painted in its own colours - its background, its accent, a sentence and a keycap
- so a theme is chosen by looking at it.  Choosing a card runs the same palette
picker the bar's glyph runs, so a switch takes the same path whichever way it was
started; the bar's right click opens that picker's swatch list on its own.

What the picker does, in order:

1. rewrites only the two forms that name the theme in your override file (a
   ~.bak~ of the previous file is kept, and your fonts, clone dir and every
   other override survive untouched),
2. renders the palette-dependent files in *two* Guile processes - the GTK files
   first, then everything else, including the Emacs bridge, so Emacs learns the
   theme and the fonts the same way every other surface does. The dconf keys are
   applied between the two, which is what lets the icon theme land before the
   dock is told its settings changed: the dock rebuilds its launchers when its
   file is rewritten, and a rebuild that started before dconf knew the new icon
   theme would draw yesterday's icons and keep them,
3. leaves the reloading to the surfaces themselves: Niri re-reads its config the
   moment the file changes, whelk rebuilds its bar a quarter of a second after
   ~settings.scm= is written, and the dock watches its own directory the same
   way - so nothing is signalled, nothing flickers, and nothing keeps a file in
   memory that the renderer has replaced,
4. pushes the new colours, the icon theme and the Heks Linux dconf keys,
5. puts a wallpaper from the new palette's set on the screen, because the set
   follows the theme and leaving yesterday's picture behind today's chrome looks
   like a half-finished switch,
6. asks the running Emacs to re-read the bridge heks-linux just wrote.  It is
   not told which theme to load: the bridge names it, and that name is
   heks-emacs's business,
7. says where it is while it does it, and closes the same notification with the
   result.

Step 7 is what makes the wait legible. A switch is a few seconds of work -
rendering fifteen surfaces is most of it - and a few seconds of silence reads as
a hang. So the picker opens *one* notification when it starts and edits it as it
moves on, from ~Applying palette: nord~ to ~Palette applied: nord~, and heks-toon
shows a pill of its own over the palette view: the stage the switch is in, how
long it has taken, and a bar that fills as the stages go by.  It reads that from
~${XDG_RUNTIME_DIR:-/tmp}/heks-theme-progress~, the one file the picker writes,
so a switch started from the bar shows up in the window as well.  While a switch is
running the window refuses a second one rather than starting a second rendering
of the desktop on top of the first.

Each palette in the picker is drawn as a swatch of its own four colours,
generated once with ImageMagick and cached under
~$XDG_CACHE_HOME/heks-linux/palette-swatches~ - a palette whose colours change
invalidates that cache exactly once, because the signature is baked into the
generated picker script.  heks-toon paints its own cards and needs none of that:
the same table is in its own Scheme, and the window is drawn from the palette the
desktop is wearing.

If you add a surface that embeds palette colours, add it to ~theme-surfaces~ in
~maak.scm~: that list is what a palette switch renders, in two Guile processes
rather than one per file. The GTK files sit in ~theme-gtk-surfaces~, a subset
rendered and applied first - see the note on icons above.

** The wallpaper engine

~heks-wallpaper~ is the one program that owns the background. The compositor
starts it at login, the systemd timer runs it on a calendar, the keybindings run
it by hand, and the palette picker runs it with an explicit path.

#+begin_src shell
heks-wallpaper            # a random wallpaper from the palette set (the default)
heks-wallpaper next       # the next one in the set
heks-wallpaper prev       # the previous one
heks-wallpaper restore    # re-apply the one on record, after a compositor restart
heks-wallpaper set PATH   # apply a specific image
heks-wallpaper current    # what is on screen right now
heks-wallpaper palette-list   # the wallpapers curated for the current palette
heks-wallpaper all-list DIR   # everything under DIR
#+end_src

~heks-toon walls~, bound to ~Mod+Shift+W~, is the interactive half: the whole
collection in the wall chooser view of heks-toon, one GTK4 window that also holds
the keyboard reference and the palette chooser. The palette's own set comes
first with the one on screen marked, the rest of the collection follows, and the
picture you select is shown large before you commit to it.

| Key or gesture        | What it does                                  |
|-----------------------+-----------------------------------------------|
| ~Type~                | Filter by file name or by folder              |
| ~↑ ↓ ← →~             | Move the selection; the preview follows it    |
| ~Enter~, double-click | Put the wallpaper on the screen - on the wall |
|                       | or on the picture in the preview              |
| ~Ctrl+1~ ~Ctrl+2~ ~Ctrl+3~ | Keys, walls, palettes: the three views   |
| ~Ctrl+F~, ~/~         | Search the view you are looking at            |
| ~Esc~                 | Close the window                              |

The window does not own the wallpaper: every choice it makes is handed to
~heks-wallpaper~, the same engine the timer and the keybindings use, and it reads
what is on the wall from the engine's own state file. Nothing is served and
nothing is cached by a server in the middle - the thumbnails it draws are
generated in the window and cached under ~$XDG_CACHE_HOME/heks-toon~. It arrives
focused, which takes a Niri window rule because the window maps without asking
for activation - without it the chooser opens behind whatever you were reading,
and a click on the bar looks like it did nothing. The rule comes from
~heks-toon --niri-rule~ and ~src/heks-linux/niri.scm~ renders it into the
session, blur included.

Three things are worth knowing:

- *Rotation is every ninety minutes.* ~heks-wallpaper.timer~ fires a change
  ninety minutes after the session's timer starts and every ninety minutes after
  that, with a five minute random delay, so the wallpaper turns over while the
  machine is being used.  It is a monotonic interval rather than a calendar
  entry because a calendar cannot say "every ninety minutes": its minute step
  has to divide an hour.
- *The renderer is pluggable.* ~wallpaper-backend~ is ~auto~ by default, which
  tries ~swaybg~ and falls back to ~wbg~; the latter is the layer-shell-only
  setter from the Niri world and needs nothing from wlroots.
- *Local overrides work like every other module.* Put
  ~(wallpaper-backend 'wbg)~, ~(wallpaper-scaling "fit")~ or
  ~(wallpaper-rotation-interval "45m")~ in
  ~$XDG_CONFIG_HOME/heks-linux/wallpaper-settings.scm~ and re-run
  ~maak install-wallpapers~.


** Music: the rmpc keys

RMPC is driven with Emacs' keys wherever rmpc has the action, and with rmpc's
own where Emacs has no equivalent.  The point is that the fingers that move
through a buffer move through the queue.

| Key            | Emacs meaning              | In rmpc                       |
|----------------+----------------------------+-------------------------------|
| ~C-n~ / ~C-p~  | ~next-line~ / ~previous-line~ | Down / Up                   |
| ~C-f~ / ~C-b~  | ~forward-char~ / ~backward-char~ | Right / Left            |
| ~C-v~ / ~M-v~  | ~scroll-up-command~ / ~scroll-down-command~ | Page down / Page up |
| ~C-a~ / ~C-e~  | ~move-beginning-of-line~ / ~move-end-of-line~ | Top / Bottom |
| ~C-s~          | ~isearch-forward~           | Search                        |
| ~C-r~          | ~isearch-backward~          | Previous result               |
| ~C-g~          | ~keyboard-quit~             | Close the pane or menu        |
| ~C-SPC~        | ~set-mark-command~          | Invert the selection          |
| ~C-k~          | ~kill-line~                 | Delete the current row        |
| ~C-l~          | ~recenter-top-bottom~       | Jump back to the playing song |
| ~C-x C-c~      | ~save-buffers-kill-emacs~   | Quit                          |
| ~C-x C-s~      | ~save-buffer~               | Save the queue as a playlist  |
| ~C-x o~        | ~other-window~              | Next tab                      |
| ~C-h k~        | ~describe-key~              | Help                          |
| ~M-x~          | ~execute-extended-command~  | Command mode                  |

The theme follows the palette: borders, the highlighted row, the current row and
the volume slider all take their colour from the palette in use, so a palette
switch repaints the player along with the rest of the desktop.
